\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agents choose replacement operations with cheap verifiers, yet
deployment can change transitions, rewards, and other agents' behavior. We
define \emph{Improvement Fidelity} for an update
$\tau=(\pi_t,\pi_{t+1})$: proxy improvement $\Delta_V$ should preserve the
sign and ordering of deployment improvement $\Delta_*$. PIVOT (Paired
Interventional Validation of Optimization Transitions) estimates the
differential correction $\Delta_*-\Delta_V$ with paired high-fidelity (HF)
interventions and allocates a fixed query budget by a one-step
knowledge-gradient rule per unit cost.

Across controlled and sealed response worlds, we separate three phenomena
that are often conflated: deployment response, update-level fidelity error,
and decision-changing error. Large response can sharply increase
proxy--deployment discrepancy without creating an allocation advantage. In
90 sealed Leduc, Kuhn, and Melting Pot roots, proxy and deployment top-1
updates differ in 51 roots; nevertheless, the descriptive normalized
PIVOT--Uniform gain is not larger on flipped roots. The primary allocation
contrast is positive in Leduc (0.036, 95\% CI [0.014, 0.064]), near-null in
Kuhn, and unresolved in Melting Pot, with mixed secondary conditions. A
sealed-query audit finds that PIVOT's query set intersects the proxy/deployment
top-1 pair in most roots, but the queried noise relative to the proxy top-2
margin can remain large. Thus response magnitude is not decision relevance,
and decision relevance is not validation value: PIVOT-KG can help when HF
evidence resolves uncertainty that matters for the update decision.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Self-improvement is usually written as a loop: generate candidates, score
them, promote one, and repeat. A self-improving agent ultimately deploys a
policy replacement, not an isolated policy value. In a trading system, a
larger order changes liquidity; in an auction, a bid changes rivals'
responses; in a recommender, a policy changes future user behavior. A verifier
can therefore rank policies correctly while ranking improvements incorrectly.
High-fidelity deployment tests can expose the error, but they are often too
costly to run on every candidate.

We study the transition $\tau_t=(\pi_t,\pi_{t+1})$. Let $V$ be a cheap
observer or replay world and let $*$ denote the policy-induced deployment
world. Define
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_V=J_V(\pi')-J_V(\pi),\qquad
  \Delta_*=\mathcal J(\pi')-\mathcal J(\pi).
\end{equation}
An improvement reversal, $\Delta_V>0$ but $\Delta_*<0$, is one salient
failure mode. More generally, deployment can change the sign or ordering of
candidate improvements, and a fidelity error matters to selection when it
changes the selected update or displaces the no-update action.
Figure~\ref{fig:reversal} shows the basic sign failure.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig1_improvement_reversal.png}
  \caption{Paired proxy and deployment improvements in the two controlled
  worlds. The lower-right quadrant is an improvement reversal. Points are a
  deterministic display subsample; IRR is computed on all transition rows.}
  \label{fig:reversal}
\end{figure}

We distinguish three response layers. The \emph{direct} layer holds the
incumbent world fixed; the \emph{actor} layer lets the environment respond
mechanically to the candidate; the \emph{strategic} layer additionally lets
other agents adapt. These correspond to progressively stronger deployment
responses.

We call the agreement between proxy and deployment improvement over the
updates an agent actually proposes \emph{Improvement Fidelity}. It is
operator-relative: the relevant population is the set of updates generated by
the self-improvement process. Because only some fidelity errors change the
update decision, limited high-fidelity evaluation should be targeted to
uncertainty that matters for selection.

Our contributions are:
\begin{description}
  \item[Contribution 1.] We formalize operator-relative Improvement Fidelity
  for self-improving agents and show why accurate global policy evaluation can
  still fail on the updates an agent actually proposes. Our analysis links
  these failures to operator shift, deployment response, and candidate
  margins.

  \item[Contribution 2.] We introduce PIVOT-KG, a paired differential
  validator that allocates a limited high-fidelity evaluation budget according
  to the expected reduction in update-selection regret per unit cost.

  \item[Contribution 3.] We evaluate Improvement Fidelity across controlled
  direct, actor, and strategic response worlds and held-out decision roots,
  showing when response creates update-level fidelity errors, when those
  errors change the selected update, and when targeted high-fidelity
  validation reduces selection regret.
\end{description}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Performative and strategic response.}
Performative prediction and reinforcement learning make deployment part of
the data-generating process
\cite{perdomo2020performative,mandal2023performative,rank2024performative,
mandal2025linear,gois2025performative,multiagentperformative2022}.
These works study learning, stability, and equilibrium behavior under
deployment-induced response; we ask whether a particular proposed update
preserves its sign and ordering after that response is realized.

\paragraph{World models and policy evaluation.}
World models and policy-aware simulators evaluate values, rankings, or
trajectory fidelity
\cite{worldgym2025,policyaware2026,tdmpc22024}.
Global value or ranking fidelity can coexist with a wrong local update sign,
which is the gap measured here.

\paragraph{Self-improvement and verification.}
Autonomous policy evolution and verification make iterative self-improvement
operational \cite{evopolicygym2026,dgm2025,selfauthored2026,wu2024progress}.
We focus on a complementary failure mode: a fixed external verifier can
evaluate the wrong response world, so a proposed replacement need not remain
an improvement after deployment.

\paragraph{Adaptive selection and paired evaluation.}
Active learning, knowledge gradients, and best-arm identification allocate
expensive observations \cite{settles2009active,lindley1956information,
frazier2009kg,chick2001selection,kalyanakrishnan2012pac}. PIVOT changes the
loss from global prediction error to update-selection regret and uses paired
rollouts to cancel common-mode variation.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\paragraph{Policy-induced worlds and response levels.}
Let $\M[\pi]$ denote the deployment world induced by policy $\pi$, including
any environment response to that policy. The corresponding deployment value is
\begin{equation}
  \J(\pi)=J(\pi;\M[\pi]).
\end{equation}
We consider three response levels:
\begin{align}
 \Delta_{\mathrm{direct}} &= J(\pip;\M[\pii])-J(\pii;\M[\pii]),\\
 \Delta_{\mathrm{actor}} &= J(\pip;\M[\pip])-J(\pii;\M[\pii]),\\
 \Delta_{\mathrm{strategic}} &=
 J_i(\pip,R_{-i}(\pip))-J_i(\pii,R_{-i}(\pii)).
\end{align}
The direct level holds the incumbent world fixed, the actor level allows the
environment to respond to the candidate, and the strategic level additionally
allows other agents to adapt. Here $R_{-i}$ denotes a general response map for
the other agents; best response is one special case.

\paragraph{Improvement Fidelity.}
Let an improvement operator $\mathcal A$ induce a transition law
$Q_{\mathcal A}$ over proposed updates $\tau=(\pi,\pi')$. For loss $L$, we
define operator-relative Improvement Fidelity as
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{IF}(V,\mathcal A;L)=
  \E_{(\pi,\pi')\sim Q_{\mathcal A}}
  [L(\Delta_V,\Delta_*)].
\end{equation}
The relevant population is therefore the updates the agent actually proposes,
rather than the policy space in general. Improvement Fidelity is an
update-level quantity: accurate policy values or rankings do not by themselves
guarantee accurate improvement estimates.

\paragraph{Core metrics.}
For transition rows, we measure the magnitude of fidelity error by
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IDE}=
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_k |\Delta_{V,k}-\Delta_{*,k}|,
\end{equation}
and let $\mathrm{ISC}$ denote sign agreement after removing ties. A salient
sign failure is the improvement reversal rate
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IRR}=P(\Delta_*<0\mid\Delta_V>0).
\end{equation}
For a candidate set $C_t$, the decision consequence is measured by
improvement-selection regret,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ISR}_t=
  \max_{j\in C_t}\Delta_{*,t,j}-\Delta_{*,t,\hat j},
\end{equation}
where $\hat j$ is the selected candidate. Thus IDE measures error magnitude,
ISC and IRR measure sign preservation, and ISR measures the consequence for
update selection.

\section{Properties of Improvement Fidelity}

The following results characterize when policy-level evaluation controls
update-level fidelity, how operator shift and deployment response can break
that connection, and what a high-fidelity validator must resolve.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform value fidelity is sufficient]
If $\sup_\pi|J_V(\pi)-\J(\pi)|\leq\varepsilon$, then
$|\dproxy-\dtrue|\leq2\varepsilon$. The sign is preserved whenever
$|\dtrue|>2\varepsilon$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\dproxy-\dtrue=[J_V(\pip)-\J(\pip)]-[J_V(\pii)-\J(\pii)]$;
the triangle inequality gives the bound and the sign statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Global policy accuracy need not imply update fidelity]
For every $\varepsilon>0$, there are a finite policy family, a verifier, a
policy distribution, and an update operator with policy MAE and rank error
below $\varepsilon$, but $\mathrm{IRR}=1$ and $\mathrm{ISC}=0$ on the
operator's transition law.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $n$ policies with $\J(i)=i/(n-1)$ and swap one adjacent pair in $V$.
Then $\mathrm{MAE}=2/[n(n-1)]$ and
$1-\mathrm{Spearman}=12/[n(n^2-1)]$, both tending to zero. Let the operator
sample only the swapped directed transition and propose the lower-valued true
policy. Its proxy sign is positive and its deployment sign is negative.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Operator Shift Bound]
Let $P$ be a calibration law, let $Q_{\mathcal A}\ll P$ have density ratio
$w=q_{\mathcal A}/p$, and let $\ell(\tau)=L(\Delta_V,\Delta_*)$ have finite
variance under $P$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \E_{Q_{\mathcal A}}[\ell]
 \leq
 \E_P[\ell]
 +\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_P(\ell)\,
 \chi^2(Q_{\mathcal A}\Vert P)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write
$\E_{Q_{\mathcal A}}[\ell]-\E_P[\ell]
=\E_P[(w-1)(\ell-\E_P[\ell])]$.
Cauchy--Schwarz and
$\E_P[(w-1)^2]=\chi^2(Q_{\mathcal A}\Vert P)$
give the result.
\end{proof}

Deployment response provides a second source of update-level error. From the
definitions in the previous section,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{\mathrm{actor}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{direct}}
 =
 J(\pip;\M[\pip])-J(\pip;\M[\pii]).
\end{equation}
Thus the actor--direct gap is exactly the change in the candidate's value
caused by the world it induces. The strategic level extends this response to
adaptive opponents through $R_{-i}$.

\begin{lemma}[Decision preservation]\label{lem:decision-preservation}
For candidates $i,j$ with true margin
$m=\Delta_{*,i}-\Delta_{*,j}>0$, if each estimated improvement has absolute
error below $m/2$, then the estimated ordering of $i$ and $j$ is unchanged.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The perturbation of the estimated margin has absolute value below $m$, so the
estimated margin remains positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Paired variance]
For a paired rollout difference $Y'-Y$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(Y'-Y)
  =
  \operatorname{Var}(Y')
  +\operatorname{Var}(Y)
  -2\operatorname{Cov}(Y',Y).
\end{equation}
When shared randomness induces positive covariance, paired evaluation has
lower variance than independent evaluation with the same marginal variances.
\end{lemma}

Together, these results narrow the validation problem. Uniformly accurate
policy values control update error, but small global MAE or rank error need
not protect the transitions generated by the improvement operator. Operator
shift and deployment response can therefore create local fidelity errors,
while only errors large relative to candidate margins can change the selected
update. Paired high-fidelity rollouts reduce the variance of estimating these
differences when common randomness induces positive covariance. This motivates
PIVOT-KG: allocate a limited high-fidelity budget to update uncertainty that
can affect selection.

\section{PIVOT-KG}

The preceding results suggest a simple validation principle: spend expensive
high-fidelity evaluations on update uncertainty that can change the selection
decision. We implement this principle with PIVOT-KG. Given an incumbent and
$K$ candidate updates, the cheap verifier first provides proxy improvements
$\{\Delta_{V,j}\}_{j=1}^K$.

Rather than model deployment improvement from scratch, PIVOT-KG models the
differential correction
\begin{equation}
  G_j=\Delta_{*,j}-\Delta_{V,j}.
\end{equation}
At the candidate level, we place a joint Gaussian prior over the correction
vector,
\begin{equation}
  G\sim\mathcal N(\mu,K_{\mathrm{prior}}),\qquad
  \Delta_*\mid\Delta_V\sim
  \mathcal N(\Delta_V+\mu,K_{\mathrm{prior}}).
\end{equation}
The prior mean, covariance, and observation variances are estimated once from
a disjoint calibration set of paired proxy/high-fidelity transitions. Thus a
new held-out decision root starts from the calibrated posterior
$\Delta_V+\mu$, without using its high-fidelity outcomes.

For a queried candidate $j$, let $Y_j$ denote its paired high-fidelity
improvement observation,
\begin{equation}
  Y_j=\Delta_{*,j}+\epsilon_j,
  \qquad
  \epsilon_j\sim\mathcal N(0,s_j^2),
\end{equation}
where $s_j^2$ is estimated from the calibration data. The incumbent and
candidate are evaluated under the same initial state, exogenous randomness,
and opponent initialization. Each query updates the joint posterior by
Gaussian conditioning; with a non-diagonal $K_{\mathrm{prior}}$, evidence can
also update correlated unqueried candidates.

We augment the candidate set with a no-update action,
$C^+=\{0,1,\ldots,K\}$, where $\Delta_{*,0}=0$ denotes retaining the
incumbent. Let $D$ denote the paired high-fidelity evidence collected at the
current decision root. The current decision is
\begin{equation}
  a(D)=\arg\max_{j\in C^+}
  \E[\Delta_{*,j}\mid D].
\end{equation}
Its posterior expected simple regret is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal R(D)=
  \E\left[
    \max_{j\in C^+}\Delta_{*,j}
    -\Delta_{*,a(D)}
    \,\middle|\,D
  \right].
\end{equation}
For a hypothetical high-fidelity observation $Y_j$, define
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EVSI}_j
  =
  \mathcal R(D)
  -
  \E_{Y_j\mid D}
  \bigl[\mathcal R(D\cup\{Y_j\})\bigr],
  \qquad
  A_j=\frac{\mathrm{EVSI}_j}{c_j}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathrm{EVSI}_j$ measures how much querying candidate $j$ is expected to
reduce update-selection regret, while dividing by $c_j$ favors the largest
expected decision improvement per unit of high-fidelity evaluation cost.
This one-step expected value of sample information gives the
knowledge-gradient acquisition used by PIVOT-KG.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PIVOT-KG under a fixed high-fidelity budget}
\label{alg:pivot-kg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Incumbent $\pi$; candidates $\{\pi'_j\}_{j=1}^K$;
calibrated correction prior $p(G)$; query costs $\{c_j\}_{j=1}^K$;
HF budget $B$
\ENSURE Selected candidate or incumbent
\STATE Evaluate each candidate with the cheap verifier to obtain
$\{\Delta_{V,j}\}_{j=1}^K$
\STATE Initialize $D\leftarrow\emptyset$, queried set
$Q\leftarrow\emptyset$, and remaining budget $b\leftarrow B$
\STATE Include the no-update action $j=0$ with $\Delta_{*,0}=0$
\WHILE{there exists $j\notin Q$ with $c_j\leq b$}
  \STATE Update $p(\Delta_*\mid\Delta_V,D)$ by Gaussian conditioning
  \FOR{each feasible unqueried candidate $j$}
    \STATE Compute $\mathrm{EVSI}_j$ and
    $A_j\leftarrow\mathrm{EVSI}_j/c_j$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $j_q\leftarrow\arg\max_j A_j$
  \STATE Obtain paired HF observation $Y_{j_q}$ under matched randomness
  \STATE $D\leftarrow D\cup\{Y_{j_q}\}$,
         $Q\leftarrow Q\cup\{j_q\}$,
         $b\leftarrow b-c_{j_q}$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \RETURN
$\displaystyle a(D)=\arg\max_{j\in C^+}\E[\Delta_{*,j}\mid D]$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The primary comparison uses this fixed-budget rule; posterior-based early
stopping is a secondary variant. Unlike global label acquisition, PIVOT-KG
values a high-fidelity query by its expected reduction in update-selection
regret.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Experimental protocol}
The experiments answer four questions rather than treating each benchmark as a
separate win report. Controlled response worlds use paired common-random-number
rollouts and 30 independent seeds. The seed, trajectory, opponent-seed cluster,
or held-out root is the inferential unit; transition rows are retained for audit
and are not treated as independent replicates. The supplied sealed archive
contains 30 held-out roots in each of Leduc, Kuhn, and Melting Pot, with 1,800
sealed decisions per cohort. We use Leduc and Kuhn poker through
OpenSpiel~\cite{lanctot2019openspiel} and the Melting Pot multi-agent
suite~\cite{leibo2021meltingpot}. Manuscript generation verifies hashes, seals,
and scored-row preservation; it does not rerun an episode.

\subsection{What breaks improvement fidelity?}
The observer/direct, actor, and strategic layers isolate progressively stronger
response mechanisms. The operator-shift study is a complementary failure
source: its high-minus-low shift contrast in operator-relative IDE is
\OperatorShiftEffect{} with bootstrap interval \OperatorShiftEffectCI{}
across \OperatorShiftSeeds{} seeds and \OperatorShiftRows{} paired rows
(Figure~\ref{fig:operator-shift}). Local update error rises while global
policy rank remains comparatively stable. Together these results distinguish
distributional operator shift from policy-induced deployment response; either
can separate proxy and deployment improvement without implying that every
operator or every update fails.

\subsection{When does fidelity error change the decision?}
\Cref{lem:decision-preservation} gives the bridge: if differential error is below half the true
candidate margin, the ordering is preserved. For each sealed root we therefore
compute the deployment top-1 margin, the proxy-to-deployment correction, and
the largest pairwise correction-to-margin ratio. A ratio at least one marks a
proxy/deployment top-1 flip; it is a decision-relevance diagnostic, not an
allocation outcome.

Across the \RevisionDecisionRoots{} sealed roots, \RevisionDecisionFlips{}
have a top-1 flip (\RevisionLeducFlips{} Leduc, \RevisionKuhnFlips{} Kuhn, and
\RevisionMeltingFlips{} Melting Pot). To avoid comparing reward scales across
worlds, Figure~\ref{fig:decision-relevance} reports each root's squared
response gap after division by the square of its largest candidate range across
proxy and long/short deployment deltas. The resulting within-root normalized
gaps are \RevisionLeducNormalizedResponseGap{}
\RevisionLeducNormalizedResponseGapCI{} for Leduc,
\RevisionKuhnNormalizedResponseGap{} \RevisionKuhnNormalizedResponseGapCI{}
for Kuhn, and \RevisionVFourNormalizedResponseGap{}
\RevisionVFourNormalizedResponseGapCI{} for Melting Pot; native-unit gaps
remain in the audit.
Yet flip status alone does not predict validation value: normalized allocation
gain is \RevisionFlippedNormalizedGain{} \RevisionFlippedNormalizedGainCI{}
on flipped roots and \RevisionStableNormalizedGain{}
\RevisionStableNormalizedGainCI{} on stable roots. Figure~\ref{fig:decision-relevance}
therefore supports the sharper claim: response can create decision-relevant
error, but decision relevance is not sufficient for an allocation advantage.

We then ask whether the sealed HF queries could resolve the fragile decision.
For each root, the ex-post decision-critical pair is the union of the proxy and
deployment top-1 candidates, while the proxy top-2 margin measures how much
uncertainty separates the leading proxy candidates. At the primary long
budget, the PIVOT query set intersects this pair in

\subsection{Stress Tests Beyond Controlled Environments}
The public finance audit uses historical paths, virtual fills, and a
participation/depth proxy. It has 0/7 depth-proxy reversals in the expanded
primary sessions and 0/5 in the holdout; the pooled depth-minus-replay effect
is approximately $-4.16\times10^{-7}$ in native session units. This is a
boundary diagnostic, not causal market impact. The strategic mechanisms are
finite fixtures rather than general equilibrium, and external adaptive worlds
are endpoint evidence rather than connected trajectories.

\section{Discussion and scope}
The evidence supports a conditional claim. Deployment response can increase
proxy--deployment error; an error changes the update only when it is large
relative to the candidate margin; and PIVOT-KG can help when limited HF queries
resolve that decision-relevant uncertainty. The sealed-query audit makes the
last condition testable at the level of observable query criticality and
residual separation, while stopping short of an unrecorded posterior or EVSI
claim. A large response gap, a top-1 flip, or a positive result in one cohort
is not by itself an allocation guarantee.
The study does not claim universal superiority, simulator ground truth, market
causality, or equilibrium behavior.

HighwayEnv reinforces the scope limit: the original one-query contrast is
unfavorable, and the primary two-query comparison is unresolved in both
cohorts. The original four-query advantage is also unresolved in the
fresh-seed replication. The budget-dependent pattern is thus exploratory;
these data establish neither a reproducible four-query advantage nor a
budget-sufficiency mechanism.

\section{Conclusion}
An agent can be correct about the wrong world. The relevant statistical object
for self-improvement is therefore the transition $\pi\to\pi'$, and the core
question is whether its sign and ordering survive deployment-induced response.
Across controlled and sealed worlds, deployment response separates proxy and
deployment improvements, but the size of that separation does not by itself
predict a ranking flip or an allocation gain. In the sealed study, 51 of 90
roots changed their proxy/deployment top-1, yet flipped roots did not have a
larger normalized PIVOT--Uniform gain.

PIVOT-KG targets the next link in this chain: whether limited HF evidence can
resolve a decision-relevant uncertainty before an update is promoted. Its
positive Leduc contrast, near-null Kuhn result, and mixed or unresolved Melting
Pot conditions support a conditional claim rather than universal superiority.
The no-update action and paired evidence are part of the decision problem, so
the method can retain the incumbent when all estimated improvements are weak or
negative. The claims remain bounded by fixed response worlds, held-out sealed
roots, and finite HF budgets; strategic fixtures are not equilibrium results,
and the finance audit is not causal market evidence.

The broader implication is to validate consequences under the response induced
by the proposed update. The resulting evidence ladder is
\begin{equation}
  \text{observer} \longrightarrow \text{actor} \longrightarrow \text{strategic},
\end{equation}
Each arrow is an additional intervention, not an assumed truth claim; an HF
budget matters only when it can change the update decision.\label{main:end}

\paragraph{HighwayEnv replication protocol.}\label{app:highway-replication}
The original and replication cohorts each contain 20 calibration and 60 test
roots, with no overlap between cohorts. The replication freezes the five-action
panel, four lanes, 20 traffic vehicles, horizon 20, and covariance shrinkage
0.5. It refits the correction prior on its own calibration cohort before
opening the full test cohort. The primary contrast is Uniform minus PIVOT-KG
ISR at $B=2$; $B=4$ is secondary. We retain every registered root and use 4,000
paired bootstrap resamples with fixed RNG seeds. Individual 95\% intervals
condition on each fitted calibration model; they do not include calibration
sampling uncertainty or form a simultaneous confidence statement.
A technical smoke run exposed
the first two replication test roots before the full run; those roots are
retained without subsequent tuning or exclusions. This is therefore an
external DEV replication, not a wholly blinded confirmatory test. The
supplement includes the execution note, complete seed records, query and truth
ledgers, dependency lock, frozen simulator code, and a standalone table/figure
recomputation command. Calibration and post-decision truth audits are outside
the logical query budget and are included in physical evaluation accounting.
Uniform HF retains proxy estimates for unqueried candidates, whereas PIVOT-KG
uses its calibrated posterior. The contrast therefore includes calibration
and final selection as well as acquisition; it does not isolate an acquisition
mechanism.
